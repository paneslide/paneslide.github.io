Most new PaneSlide players lose swipes in the same few ways. None of them are about being "bad at puzzles". They are habits that feel natural with touch screens but work against a game where every swipe counts. Here are the seven we see most often, and a simple fix for each.

## 1. Swiping before reading the picture

It is tempting to start moving glass straight away to "see what happens". In PaneSlide that usually costs swipes, because a pane often stops somewhere you did not expect, and getting it back costs more swipes.

**Instead:** spend ten seconds on the small target picture first. For each cell, name the colour. Plain cyan, magenta or yellow means one pane. Blue (cyan + magenta), green (cyan + yellow) and red (magenta + yellow) mean two panes overlap. Black means all three. An empty cell means no glass. The [reading the picture guide](/guides/reading-the-picture/) has a step-by-step method.

## 2. Expecting the pane to stop where your finger stops

PaneSlide panes glide. However short or long your swipe is, the pane keeps going until the edge, a blocker, or new colour stops it. Short careful swipes do not give more control.

**Instead:** predict the stop before you swipe. Trace the pane's rail with your eyes and find the first thing that will stop it. The [glide and stick guide](/guides/how-panes-move/) lists every rule.

## 3. Forgetting that new colour makes glass stick

The second rule surprises many players. When a gliding pane slides onto a cell where it would overlap more crossing glass than before, it stops right there. So a pane you wanted at the far edge can stop halfway, on top of another pane.

**Instead:** before swiping, look along the rail for crossing panes. The first one in the way is usually where your pane will stop. If you want it to go further, move the crossing pane out of the way first, or check whether the overlap is on ice, where glass never sticks.

## 4. Moving panes that are already right

If a pane is already in its final spot, moving it away and back costs at least two swipes. Many beginners nudge every pane once "to check".

**Instead:** compare the board with the picture first, and mark the panes that already match. Leave them alone unless they are in the way of something else. The move counter shows par next to your swipes. If par equals the number of panes that need to move, each of those panes needs exactly one swipe.

## 5. Not using one pane to stop another

On a bare board, a pane can only stop at the edge. To stop a pane in the middle, you need something there: a pillar, sand, locked glass, a pane on the same kind of rail, or a crossing pane it will stick on. Beginners often try to place panes one by one, in any order, and get stuck when a pane will not stop where it should.

**Instead:** ask "what will stop this pane in the right place?" If the answer is another pane, move that one first. Order is the heart of PaneSlide. The [plan your swipes guide](/guides/plan-your-swipes/) shows how to build the order, often by working backwards.

## 6. Treating undo as a free rewind

Undo is always there in levels and in dailies from 30 October 2026, but it **does not give swipes back**. A swipe followed by an undo still adds one to your count. Players who try lots of ideas with undo often finish with one or two stars when they could have had three.

**Instead:** use undo when a swipe clearly went wrong, so you do not build on a mistake. For testing ideas, think first. If you really cannot decide, a hint is often better value: on levels, hints never cost stars.

## 7. Chasing three stars on the first try

Three stars need par or par + 1 swipes (exactly par on the very first levels, where par is 1 or 2). On hard levels, some players restart again and again for three stars and stop enjoying the puzzle.

**Instead:** solve it first, however many swipes it takes. Every solve earns at least one star, and you can replay any level from the map later. Stars only ever go up. Once you have seen the solution, three stars are much easier on a second go.

## A simple routine to replace all seven

1. **Read** the picture, cell by cell.
2. **Mark** the panes that are already right.
3. **Ask** what will stop each remaining pane in the right place.
4. **Order** the swipes so every stopper is ready in time.
5. **Swipe**, then compare with your plan. Undo only if something went wrong.

It takes about half a minute on a small board and saves far more than that. With a little practice it becomes automatic, and you will notice your stars climbing.

> **Tip.** Not sure where a pane will stop? On a laptop or desktop, hover over a pane to highlight it (locked glass stays plain), and use the arrow keys for precise single swipes. See [keyboard and mouse controls](/guides/keyboard-and-mouse/).
